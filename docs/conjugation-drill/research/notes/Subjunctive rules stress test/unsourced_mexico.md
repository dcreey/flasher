## 1. "¿Crees que venga?" (plain question, creer + subjunctive) is more common in Mexico

### Takeaway
**Still no evidence.** I found no corpus study or dialect study that compares *¿crees que* + subjunctive across countries. The only support for a Mexican preference is anecdotal (one Quora answer, plus forum claims). The one empirical Mexico City study I found a trace of, Blake 1981, points the other way for doubt contexts in general, though it isn't about questions.

### Cited Findings
- A Quora answer by one native speaker says *¿Crees que esto signifique…?* "se dice en México", is not used in many other Latin American countries, and that Spain normally uses the indicative. This is one person's impression. — [Quora (es)](https://es.quora.com/Cu%C3%A1ndo-se-usa-el-Subjuntivo-en-preguntas-He-o%C3%ADdo-cosas-como-por-ejemplo-que-en-una-serie-dicen-Crees-que-esto-signifique-mientras-en-los-subt%C3%ADtulos-se-pod%C3%ADa-leer-Crees-que-esto-significa-Qu%C3%A9) (L, seen only through a search summary, so SN)
- A CVC forum post says that with an affirmative *¿Crees…?* the subjunctive tilts the question toward an expected negative answer (*¿Crees que sean inteligentes?*). It names no region. — [CVC foro 53283](https://cvc.cervantes.es/foros/leer_asunto.asp?vCodigo=53283) (L, via search summary; the site was down)
- In a negative question (*¿No crees que…?*) the indicative is the norm, and the subjunctive there is rare and signals surprise. — [WordReference, "Por qué crees que + subjuntivo"](https://forum.wordreference.com/threads/por-qu%C3%A9-crees-que-subjuntivo.2343404/) (L, via search summary)
- Earlier run: forum users on Tomisimo and Kwiziq say the choice "depends on region" or is "most common in Mexico", with no data. — see noun_clauses.md, Q4 (L)
- Counter-evidence (weak): Blake (1981), "Some empirically based observations on adult usage of the subjunctive mood in Mexico City" (in Lantolf & Stone eds., *Current Research in Romance Languages*, IULC, pp. 13–22). A secondary summary says a significant share of participants, more in Mexico than in Spain, preferred the *indicative* in subjunctive contexts such as doubt. This concerns doubt predicates, not questions. — [search summary pointing to the Faulkner 2021 Pragmatics article and others](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/350297337_Prescriptively_or_descriptively_speaking_How_%27information-quality%27_influences_mood_variation_in_Spanish_emotive-factive_clauses) (SN; paraphrased, original not seen)

### Inferences
- The construction is grammatical everywhere: a question is a non-assertive context. The subjunctive adds doubt or expects "no". A Mexican skew is plausible but rests only on anecdote.
- Corrected doc wording (§5 table row and §13): "Plain question: *¿Crees que…?* usually takes the indicative. The subjunctive adds doubt or expects a 'no' (*¿Crees que venga?*). Speakers often say it is more common in Mexico, but no study confirms this (L)." Keep the app accepting both and generating the indicative.

### Gaps
- No CORPES XXI or Corpus del Español count of *crees que* + subjunctive by country. This could be measured directly with a CORPES query (`crees que` + present subjunctive, filtered by country) but I can't run it from here.
- Blake 1981 was not seen first-hand.

## 2. "No sé qué haga" (indirect question + subjunctive) is colloquial and growing in Mexico

### Takeaway
**Partly confirmed, and the doc's framing is off.** Mexico City speech has real indicative/subjunctive variation after *no sé* + *qué/si/quién*. That variation is absent from modern Madrid. In Mexico it is **not** tied to colloquial style, and most Mexican cases are *different-subject* indirect questions (*No sé si los protejan*), not the same-subject deliberative *No sé qué haga*. The deliberative type (*No sé qué te diga*, *No sé si salga*) is described in peninsular grammars too (Bello, GDLE). The evidence for "growing" is only preliminary (SN). Two sources conflict on how dominant the subjunctive is in Mexico City.

### Cited Findings
- **Nieuwenhuijsen, Dorien (2001), "Modo verbal en las oraciones interrogativas indirectas", NRFH XLIX(2), 339–362.** Corpora: *El habla de la Ciudad de México* (Lope Blanch 1971, educated speakers), *El habla popular de la Ciudad de México* (Lope Blanch 1976), Rulfo, 16th-century New Spain letters, and *El Periquillo* (1816). The construction is indirect questions after negated *saber*. — [Redalyc PDF](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/602/60249203.pdf) (F, read in full)
  - Modern peninsular (Madrid educated speech, Pérez-Reverte): 88 indicative, **0 subjunctive (0%)** (Table 2). (F)
  - Modern Mexico (Table 3): Mexico City educated speakers 28 indicative / 7 subjunctive = **20%**; Mexico City popular speakers 45 / 13 = **22%**; Rulfo 21 / 1 = 5%; total 94 / 21 = **18% subjunctive**. "existe variación … si bien hay una clara preferencia por el indicativo". (F)
  - "no hay diferencia entre los hablantes cultos y populares … el uso de subjuntivo en las interrogativas indirectas no está regido por motivos sociolingüísticos." So it is **not** a colloquial or popular feature. (F)
  - 16th-century Mexican letters: 25% subjunctive; 15th–16th-century Spain: 25%. The variation is old and continuous. Spain lost it; Mexico kept it. This is not a new innovation. (F)
  - Tense: the subjunctive appears only with present or future reference (educated speakers 22%, popular speakers 37% of present/future cases) and **never with past reference** (0 of 10 and 0 of 26) (Tables 6–7). (F)
  - Same subject vs different subject (Table 5, OCR-garbled in the PDF; read as): educated speakers 5 indirect (different subject) vs 2 dubitative (same subject); popular speakers 11 vs 2. So most Mexican subjunctive cases are true indirect questions with a different subject. Examples: *No sé si los PROTEJAN*, *no sé qué clase de broche de oro nos TENGAN preparado*. (F; the PDF prints "110" and a total row of "320/120", which are OCR errors for 11 and 32/12)
  - Same-subject *No sé qué te DIGA* / *No sé qué decirte* is classed by the GDLE and others as "interrogativa dubitativa/deliberativa", which also admits the infinitive. Bello contrasts *No sé si salga* (decision up to the subject) with *No sé si saldré*. These are general Spanish, not Mexican. (F, as quoted by Nieuwenhuijsen)
  - Lope Blanch (1972, *Estudios sobre el español de México*, p. 145): in indirect questions Mexican speakers prefer the present subjunctive to the future, as part of the weakening of the synthetic future. (F, as quoted)
  - Moreno de Alba (1985, *Valores de las formas verbales en el español de México*, p. 135): in a questionnaire of 40 informants on *no sé* sentences, **67% indicative / 33% subjunctive** or **55% / 45%**, depending on the sentence. (F, as quoted)
- **Conflicting result:** Gerards (2024 abstract, Univ. Mainz) says the only pan-Hispanic quantitative study, DeMello (1995), "concluye que la variante con subjuntivo predomina en Ciudad de México, es minoritaria en Bogotá, Caracas y Santiago de Chile y está ausente del habla de Buenos Aires, La Habana, La Paz, Lima, Madrid, San Juan y Sevilla (cf. también NGLE 2009: 1901)". That data is from the 1960s and 70s, for *no sé si*. — [Gerards abstract, USC](https://ilg.usc.gal/sites/default/files/pdfs/resumo_gerards.pdf) (F for the abstract; DeMello 1995 not seen)
- **"Growing":** the same abstract says the CORPES XXI analysis "mostrará" that the subjunctive variant has spread considerably since the 1960s and 70s and is now documented in Peru, Cuba, Bolivia and elsewhere. This is a conference abstract with no figures. — [Gerards abstract](https://ilg.usc.gal/sites/default/files/pdfs/resumo_gerards.pdf) (F for the claim, but preliminary, so grade the growth itself SN)
- An earlier run found an ULL *Revista de Filología* article saying the use grew "en el siglo XX, y con más intensidad en el XXI, en Hispanoamérica, especialmente en algunas áreas como México", and that *no sé qué hago* ≠ *no sé qué haga*. — [ULL Revista de Filología](https://www.ull.es/revistas/index.php/filologia/article/download/7656/5256/) (SN; fetch failed again)
- RAE *Gramática básica* §25.3: indirect questions take the indicative, *No sé qué {hacer ~ haré}*; with *saber* the subjunctive is "propio solo de algunas áreas como la mexicana o la caribeña". — [RAE gramática básica 25.3](https://www.rae.es/gram%C3%A1tica-b%C3%A1sica/oraciones-subordinadas-sustantivas/interrogativas-indirectas) (SN). This conflicts with DeMello's finding that it is absent in La Habana and San Juan, possibly because of the difference in data dates.

### Inferences
- In Mexico the subjunctive after *no sé* + wh-word or *si* is a real variant: about 20% of cases in 1960s–70s Mexico City speech (Nieuwenhuijsen), and possibly the majority in DeMello's count for *no sé si*. It is found at every educational level and only for present or future reference. It replaces the future (*no sé si vendrá* → *no sé si venga*).
- The two figures conflict, possibly because of counting differences: DeMello counted *no sé si*; Nieuwenhuijsen counted all wh-words plus *si*. Neither should be stated as "the" rate.
- Same-subject *No sé qué haga* sits alongside *No sé qué hacer* everywhere as a deliberative. The infinitive is the default. No source here measures infinitive vs subjunctive in Mexico; Nieuwenhuijsen counts only indicative vs subjunctive.
- Corrected doc wording (§5/§9, and the §13 summary row): "*No sé si venga / No sé qué haga*: in Mexico the present subjunctive often replaces the future in *no sé* + *si/qué/quién* questions about the present or future (*No sé si los protejan* = I don't know if they'll protect them). In 1960s–70s Mexico City speech it was about 1 in 5 cases (educated and popular speakers alike), and it is absent in Madrid (F, Nieuwenhuijsen 2001). Another study found it the majority form in Mexico City for *no sé si* (F, via Gerards 2024 citing DeMello 1995). Never for the past. Same-subject *No sé qué haga* (≈ what should I do) also exists in Spain alongside the more usual *No sé qué hacer*. It is reported to be spreading in Latin America (SN)." Drop "colloquial". Keep "not flagged". Optionally accept the present subjunctive after *no sé si/qué* only for present or future reference.

### Gaps
- DeMello (1995) itself, with its figures and exact contexts, was not seen. Neither was NGLE 2009 p. 1901.
- Gerards's CORPES XXI results were not published in the abstract.
- No modern (PRESEEA-México) figures.

## 3. Polite "pudiera / debiera / quisiera" is more common in Mexico

### Takeaway
**Refuted for *pudiera/debiera*; *quisiera* is the preferred form, but everywhere.** Mexican data show the conditional beating the *-ra* form in polite use, 64% to 36%. The exception is *querer*, where *quisiera* predominates, and a Spain + America study finds the same pattern on both sides of the Atlantic.

### Cited Findings
- Vázquez Laslop, M. E. (2007), "'Pudiera' de dicto", NRFH LV(1), 1–22, quoting Moreno de Alba (1985, *Valores de las formas verbales en el español de México*, 2nd ed., p. 150): "En expresiones corteses o en locuciones en que el pretérito de subjuntivo es permutable por pospretérito, 12 casos. Con este valor, es más frecuente en México el uso del pospretérito, que el del pretérito de subjuntivo (64%/36%) aunque no con el verbo querer, con el que predomina el uso del pretérito de subjuntivo". The sample is very small (12 cases). — [Redalyc PDF](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/602/60255101.pdf) (F)
- Same article: in the Corpus del Español Mexicano Contemporáneo (CEMC, El Colegio de México), *podría* + infinitive = 503 cases (7%) vs *pudiera* + infinitive = 194 (3%). In Mexico *pudiera* most often has an epistemic, *de dicto* sense (probability .74: "it might be that…") rather than a polite one. — [Redalyc PDF](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/602/60255101.pdf) (F)
- Same article, citing De Sterck (2000, *Registros y áreas geográficas en lingüística*, Salamanca, pp. 158 ff.): in Spain and America, spoken and written, *quisiera* > *querría*, but *podría*, *debería* > *pudiera*, *debiera*. — [Redalyc PDF](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/602/60255101.pdf) (F, as quoted; De Sterck not seen)
- Same article, footnote 38: independent *pareciera* (= *parecería*) is "muy común en el discurso periodístico"; a CREA search found 173 hits in Mexican documents (*pareciera que el gobierno federal los desconoce…*, Proceso 1997). There is no comparison with other countries, so this does not show Mexico uses it more. — [Redalyc PDF](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/602/60255101.pdf) (F)
- A WordReference poster says that in Mexico *quisiera* is the usual form in shops. — [WordReference](https://forum.wordreference.com/threads/quisiera-pedirle-un-favor.96599/) (L, via search summary)

### Inferences
- Corrected doc wording (§7): "Polite *-ra*: *Quisiera un café* is the normal polite form everywhere (more common than *querría*). *Pudiera* and *debiera* also exist, but *podría* and *debería* are more frequent, in Mexico as well (Mexico: 64% conditional vs 36% *-ra* in polite uses, Moreno de Alba; the CEMC corpus has *podría* 503 vs *pudiera* 194) (F). In Mexico, *pudiera* often means 'it might be that'. Independent *pareciera que…* is common in Mexican journalism (F)." Remove "more common in Mexico" for *pudiera/debiera*.

### Gaps
- No cross-country frequency comparison of polite *pudiera/debiera* (De Sterck 2000 not seen directly).
- Whether *pareciera que* is more frequent in Mexico than in Spain is untested (it could be checked in CORPES).

## 4. Indicative after feelings ("me alegra que viniste", "qué bueno que viniste", "me da coraje que lo hizo") in Mexico City: per-city percentages

### Takeaway
**Still no evidence for figures.** The study that has per-city figures exists: DeMello 1996, NRFH, covering five expressions of personal reaction in 12 cities including México. But I could not read it (Cloudflare 403; not on redalyc). The qualitative claim is already F: Lope Blanch restricts the indicative to real past or present facts. Blake 1981 is cited for Mexican indicative in emotive-factives, but I found no figures.

### Cited Findings
- DeMello, George (1996), "Indicativo por subjuntivo en cláusula regida por expresión de reacción personal", NRFH 44(2), 365–386, DOI [10.24201/nrfh.v44i2.1943](https://doi.org/10.24201/nrfh.v44i2.1943). Crossref metadata confirms title and DOI. — [Crossref API](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=DeMello+Indicativo+por+subjuntivo+reacci%C3%B3n+personal&rows=2) (F for the citation only)
- Snippets of that article: it compares five expressions of personal reaction in the educated-speech (Norma Culta) corpora of 12 cities (Bogotá, Buenos Aires, Caracas, La Habana, La Paz, Lima, Madrid, México, San José de Costa Rica, San Juan, Santiago de Chile, Sevilla). The frequency of the indicative varies by city. It cites Lope Blanch (1958) for indicative after verbs of emotion in Mexican Spanish ("no son raras"). Corpus examples include Lima, Santiago, Bogotá and San José (*Me agrada que esa tesis ha sido comentada…*). — [NRFH download](https://nrfh.colmex.mx/index.php/nrfh/article/download/1943/1934/) (SN; 403 on fetch)
- Faulkner (2021, *Pragmatics* 31(3)) reviews DeMello (1996) and other studies: their emotive-predicate results "either contradict or are hardly compatible with" the hypothesis that informational value predicts mood. — [JBE / Pragmatics](https://doi.org/10.1075/prag.19044.fau) (SN)
- Secondary sources cite Blake (1981) for Mexican Spanish allowing the indicative in emotive-factive clauses (with Crespo del Río 2014). — [search summary, Pragmatics / Spanish in Context papers](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/350297337_Prescriptively_or_descriptively_speaking_How_%27information-quality%27_influences_mood_variation_in_Spanish_emotive-factive_clauses) (SN)
- Lope Blanch (1990, in Bosque ed., *Indicativo y subjuntivo*), as quoted: the indicative after verbs of emotion occurs only for past or present facts whose reality is asserted (*Me da coraje que lo hizo sin permiso*). — [hispanoteca](http://hispanoteca.eu/Foro/ARCHIVO-Foro/Subjuntivo%20o%20indicativo%20con%20verbos%20de%20afecci%C3%B3n.htm) (F, from noun_clauses.md; re-read in the scratch copy)
- Lastra & Martín Butragueño (Corpus sociolingüístico de la Ciudad de México) "reafirma la plena vitalidad del subjuntivo (especialmente en las personas con más estudios, los jóvenes y las mujeres)". The snippet does not isolate emotive clauses. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/260772399_Aproximacion_al_uso_del_modo_subjuntivo_en_el_Corpus_sociolinguistico_de_la_Ciudad_de_Mexico) (SN; 403)
- Mexico and Colombia keep the subjunctive better than Puerto Rico, which shows the most reduction. — [BAPLE, Puerto Rico study](https://baple.academiapr.org/index.php/baple/article/download/16927/11845/72099) (SN)

### Inferences
- Corrected doc wording (§2 and §13): "In Latin America, Mexico included, speakers sometimes use the indicative after a feeling for a real past or present fact (*Qué bueno que viniste*, *Me da coraje que lo hizo*) (F, Lope Blanch). A 12-city study (DeMello 1996) found the rate varies by city; its figures haven't been checked here. The subjunctive remains fully alive in Mexico City (SN)." Do not state any percentage.
- *Qué bueno que* + indicative: no Mexico-specific source was found. Keep it as a Latin American example under Lope Blanch's past/present-fact restriction.

### Gaps
- DeMello 1996, Tables (per-city %): not read. The user can get it via the DOI (Cloudflare blocks automated fetches).
- Blake 1981: no figures seen.
- No PRESEEA-México figures on emotive clauses.

## 5. Ojalá + imperfect subjunctive for a past event with unknown outcome in American Spanish ("Ojalá llegara bien" = "I hope he arrived well")

### Takeaway
**Still no evidence.** No dialect study, NGLE passage, or corpus count was found that ties this use to American Spanish. The support is learner sites and forum posts seen through search summaries. A university style guide (F, earlier run) gives the standard mapping (imperfect = hard to fulfil, present or future; perfect = possible recent past), which cuts against the past reading as the norm. A forum poster's remark suggests the past-unknown use exists, but its distribution is unmapped.

### Cited Findings
- A learner site says the imperfect subjunctive can express a past wish with unknown outcome: *ojalá tuvieras suerte en el examen de ayer* ("no sabemos el resultado"). — [espanolavanzado.com](https://www.espanolavanzado.com/significados/1818-ojala-significado) (L, via search summary)
- WordReference thread "Ojalá Mario tuviera suerte ayer en el examen": it quotes NGLE §42.4ñ (*ojalá* always takes the subjunctive). According to the search summary, it discusses *tuviera* for a past event with unknown result and says it is unclear which American areas use it. — [WordReference 4110822](https://forum.wordreference.com/threads/ojal%C3%A1-mario-tuviera-suerte-ayer-en-el-examen.4110822/) (L, via search summary; 418 on fetch)
- WordReference thread "Ojalá pudieras/pudiste": a user says his variety would use *ojalá hayas podido* for the past, while "many other varieties" would use *pudieras/pudieses*. No countries are named. — [WordReference 4083380](https://forum.wordreference.com/threads/ojal%C3%A1-pudieras-pudiste.4083380/) (L, via search summary)
- Kwiziq treats *ojalá llegaran a tiempo* as a less probable outcome, with no region. — [Kwiziq](https://spanish.kwiziq.com/revision/grammar/ojala-with-past-tenses-in-the-subjunctive) (L)
- UDEP's tense table: present = possible present or future; perfect = possible recent past; imperfect = hard to fulfil (present or future); pluperfect = impossible past. — [UDEP, Ojalá](https://www.udep.edu.pe/castellanoactual/ojala/) (F, from independent_tenses.md)

### Inferences
- RK: this matches a known pattern. In much of Latin America, where the preterite takes over present-perfect uses, the imperfect subjunctive is also used for past-reference subjunctives (*Ojalá llegara bien* ≈ *Ojalá haya llegado bien*). That is consistent with the forum remark but unproven here.
- Corrected doc wording: "For a past event whose outcome you don't know, the standard form is the perfect: *Ojalá haya llegado bien* (F, UDEP). Some speakers, reportedly in Latin America, also use the imperfect, *Ojalá llegara bien* (L/RK). The app should generate the perfect and may accept the imperfect when the context is clearly past." Keep the claim at L/RK.

### Gaps
- No NGLE quote found on *ojalá* + imperfect with past reference in American Spanish (the NGLE chapters on the subjunctive's time reference and on desideratives would be the place; not fetchable).
- No corpus counts, e.g. CORPES: *ojalá* + *-ra* next to *ayer* or other past adverbs, by country.
