# Colombia misreads Spain's everyday words

The words that matter most when rewriting Spain-style Spanish for Colombia are not slang. They are ordinary Spain words that exist in Colombia with a different everyday meaning, so a Colombian reads them wrongly instead of noticing they are foreign. A Spain **bocadillo** (sandwich) is guava paste in Colombia, a **coche** (car) is a baby stroller, a **plátano** (banana) is a plantain, **tinto** (red wine) is black coffee, a **canguro** (babysitter) is a kangaroo, **paro** is not the Colombian word for unemployment, and Spain's "¡qué pena!" (what a shame) reads as "how embarrassing / sorry". Behind those misreadings sits a larger set of about 100 sourced noun pairs where the Spain word is understood but sounds foreign: **ordenador → el computador** (masculine, unlike Mexico's *la computadora*), **móvil → celular**, **zumo → jugo**, **bolígrafo → esfero**, **pajita → pitillo**, **bombilla → bombillo**, **calcetín → media**, **biberón → tetero**, **currículum → hoja de vida**, **DNI → cédula**. About 25 of these pairs flip grammatical gender. Verbs are fewer. The safe rewrites are *aparcar → parquear*, *conducir → manejar*, *encender → prender*, *apetecer → provocar*, *echar de menos → extrañar* and "¿me pones…?" → "¿me regala…?". The headline verb finding reverses the Mexico rule: **coger is neutral in Colombia**, so it must not be swapped. Colombian slang (chévere, bacano, parce, guayabo, plata, pola, camellar, mamar gallo) is well attested, but it ranges from safe to offensive (chimba, marica, gonorrea), and a rewrite must keep the source's register. The audit of the app's Colombia lists finds that the word choices are mostly right, but the matching is not. Because each pair matches anywhere it is bounded by non-letters, several entries break ordinary sentences. In the Spain list, which runs on every English → Spanish translation, "vale la pena" becomes "listo la pena" and "el tercer piso" becomes "el tercer apartamento". In the input list, which runs on every Spanish → English request, "de una vez" becomes "claro vez" and "vino tinto" becomes "vino café". Three reactions on the slang card raise the register or borrow another country's slang: **qué chimba**, **no joda** and **ni de fundas**. No source places *ni de fundas* in Colombia.

Each claim below names its source. The strongest is the Instituto Caro y Cuervo learner glossary *Hecho en Colombia* by Gloria Viviana Nieto Martín (2017), cited as **ICC**. It labels entries **Co.** (used only in Colombia) or **Am.** (Latin American, used in Colombia) ([ICC](https://todoele.net/sites/default/files/2025-06/Hecho%20en%20Colombia-glosario.pdf)). The ASALE *Diccionario de americanismos* (DA) and Wiktionary regional labels come next. Learner sites and newspapers count as corroboration only. The RAE's DLE returned HTTP 403 throughout, and the Caro y Cuervo *Diccionario de colombianismos* (2018, about 6,000 entries with regional and usage marks) is not online ([El Universal](https://www.eluniversal.com.co/colombia/2018/04/16/8000-definiciones-son-parte-del-nuevo-diccionario-de-colombianismos/)). So no "Col." mark was read directly, and no corpus frequency was checked. A small translation survey from a Padova MA thesis serves only as supporting evidence. Its four Colombian informants are all from the Caribbean coast, not Bogotá, Medellín or Cali ([De Checchi 2023](https://thesis.unipd.it/retrieve/be868b08-c9be-48e6-8bb0-5387ed6d5776/DeChecchi_Janette.pdf)). Items the notes mark as the researchers' own knowledge are labelled *unverified* here.

## A dozen Spain words are read wrongly in Colombia, and they matter more than slang

A Spain word that a Colombian doesn't know is a small problem. A Spain word that a Colombian knows with another meaning is a real error. The table lists the clearest cases.

| Spain word (Spain meaning) | Colombian reading | Colombian word for the Spain meaning | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| bocadillo (sandwich) | guava paste (also Venezuela, Panama) | el sándwich / el sánduche | [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/bocadillo); ICC "bocadillo veleño, Co."; [Wiktionary sánduche](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/s%C3%A1nduche) |
| coche (car) | baby stroller | el carro | [Wiktionary coche](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/coche); [Wiktionary carro](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/carro) |
| plátano (banana) | cooking plantain | el banano | [Wiktionary banano](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/banano); [Wiktionary plátano](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/pl%C3%A1tano) |
| tinto (red wine) | small black coffee (Co.) | el vino tinto | [ICC](https://todoele.net/sites/default/files/2025-06/Hecho%20en%20Colombia-glosario.pdf) |
| paro (unemployment) | not used for unemployment | el desempleo | [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/paro); survey: every American informant said *desempleo* ([De Checchi](https://thesis.unipd.it/retrieve/be868b08-c9be-48e6-8bb0-5387ed6d5776/DeChecchi_Janette.pdf)) |
| canguro (babysitter) | kangaroo | la niñera / el niñero | [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/canguro) |
| piso (flat) | floor only | el apartamento | [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/piso) |

The ham sandwich, "un bocadillo de jamón", thus reads as ham-flavoured guava sweet. Colombian use of *paro* for a strike (*paro nacional*) is widely reported, but it went unsourced in this research. The mirror images are subtler. Spain's **pitillo** (cigarette, skinny jeans) is a drinking straw in Colombia ([Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/pitillo)). Spain's **bombillo** (lock cylinder) is the Colombian light bulb ([Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/bombillo)). Spain's **pimentón** (paprika) is the Colombian bell pepper ([Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/piment%C3%B3n)). In Colombia **torta** is a cake or pie, not a flatbread or a slap ([Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/torta)). **Droguería** is the everyday Colombian word for a pharmacy ([Cámara de Comercio de Bogotá](https://bibliotecadigital.ccb.org.co/server/api/core/bitstreams/cc6f87f4-3531-4cf1-863c-072fdc16d9cd/content)).

**Qué pena** is the most important phrase-level trap. In Colombia *pena* means embarrassment, not sorrow. "Qué pena (con usted)" is the standard courtesy formula for getting attention, apologizing or softening a refusal, as the Caro y Cuervo linguist Nieto explains ([Portafolio](https://www.portafolio.co/tendencias/sociales/que-pena-con-usted-la-expresion-en-colombia-que-confunde-a-los-extranjeros-601258)). To say "what a shame", Colombians say *qué lástima* ([How to Learn Spanish](https://howlearnspanish.com/?p=4475)). A Spain "¡Qué pena que no vengas!" must therefore become "¡Qué lástima que no vengas!", or the speaker sounds embarrassed. **Regalar** works the other way round. "¿Me regala un café?" is an ordinary paid request in Colombia, and DAMER records this request sense for central Colombia and Guatemala ([DAMER regalar](https://www.asale.org/damer/regalar); [Matador Network](https://matadornetwork.com/es/11-palabras-y-expresiones-colombianas-que-confundiran-un-espanol-si-o-si/)). Spain's "¿me pones un café?" is the natural place to apply it.

A few harmless Spain words have crude Colombian senses, which the ICC records.

| Word | Colombian sense (ICC) | Spain sense |
|---|---|---|
| bollo | Co. vulg., a lump of excrement | bun |
| turma | Co. pop., testicle; dimwit | — |
| paja | Am. inf., a lie (and vulgar for masturbation, as elsewhere) | straw |
| zorra | Co., a two-wheeled horse cart | vixen; an insult |
| joda | Co. vulg., thing | — |

Because of the first row, **bollo should never appear in Colombian output** ([ICC](https://todoele.net/sites/default/files/2025-06/Hecho%20en%20Colombia-glosario.pdf)).

**Coger** is the word to leave alone. es.wiktionary gives its neutral senses for Spain, Peru, Colombia and Cuba, and its sexual sense only for the Southern Cone, Venezuela, Mexico and Central America ([es.wiktionary](https://es.wiktionary.org/wiki/coger)). en.wiktionary says the vulgar sense is "entirely absent" in Colombia ([en.wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/coger)). The ICC's own native example sentence is "Salgo a coger la buseta" ([ICC](https://todoele.net/sites/default/files/2025-06/Hecho%20en%20Colombia-glosario.pdf)). The Colombian vulgar verb to avoid is *pichar* ([Dictionary.com](https://www.dictionary.com/e/slang/coger/)). Several other warnings are widely repeated but went unsourced in this research: *chucha* as body odour, *polla* as a betting pool, and Colombian "qué concha" meaning "what nerve", with the vulgar sense of *concha* placed in Argentina, Chile and Peru.

When the translator already produces one of these words, the right move is to leave it.

**Gender is part of the rewrite.** Spain-masculine nouns that become feminine in Colombia are calcetín → la media, frigorífico → la nevera, guisante → la arveja, neumático → la llanta, intermitente → la direccional, currículum → la hoja de vida, DNI → la cédula, cubo → la caneca and microbús → la buseta. Spain-feminine nouns that become masculine are bombilla → el bombillo, pajita → el pitillo, acera → el andén, sudadera → el buzo, gamba → el camarón, encimera → el mesón, lejía → el blanqueador, **resaca → el guayabo**, mascarilla → el tapabocas and zapatillas → los tenis. These genders come from the Wiktionary and ICC entries already cited. *Tapabocas*, *lavaplatos* and *lavamanos* are invariable in the plural ([Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tapabocas)). *El parte* is a traffic fine, while *la parte* is "part" (ICC). One source conflict remains: the ICC prints *caneca* as masculine, while Wiktionary gives it as feminine, which suggests an ICC typo ([Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/caneca)).

Some well-known pairs had no source in this research and stay unverified: **grifo → llave**, paraguas → sombrilla, **cazadora → chaqueta**, chupete → chupo, guardería → jardín, goma → borrador, celo → cinta, salón → sala, nata → crema de leche, cambio (money back) → vueltas and mochila → morral.

## Colombian verbs keep coger and add provocar, parquear and regalar

Verb differences are fewer and mostly pan-American. The ICC labels **manejar** ("Yo aprendí a manejar en un Renault 4"), **prender** ("el carro prende") and **parquear** as *Am.* with no register mark, so they are safe in any register ([ICC](https://todoele.net/sites/default/files/2025-06/Hecho%20en%20Colombia-glosario.pdf)). es.wiktionary places *parquear* in Central America and northern South America ([es.wiktionary](https://es.wiktionary.org/wiki/parquear)). **Provocar** meaning "feel like" ("me provoca ir al teatro") is colloquial in Colombia, Peru and Venezuela, and it is the Colombian replacement for Spain's *apetecer* ([es.wiktionary sense 7](https://es.wiktionary.org/wiki/provocar)). The swap must apply only to the "me provoca + infinitive or noun" construction, because *provocar* also means "provoke".

**Arrendar** (noun *arriendo*) is the standard Colombian word for renting housing in the press and at banks ([Portafolio](https://www.portafolio.co/mis-finanzas/vivienda/como-arrendar-un-inmueble-en-colombia-525508); [Bancolombia](https://blog.bancolombia.com/vida-cotidiana/como-hacer-contrato-de-arrendamiento-de-vivienda-urbana/)). The same paper also writes "alquilar vivienda" ([Portafolio](https://www.portafolio.co/economia/el-leasing-y-otras-formas-de-alquilar-vivienda-531297)), so the swap is a preference, not a correction. **Echar de menos → extrañar** is shared with Mexico ([colombianspanish.co](https://colombianspanish.co/blog/colombian-spanish-learners-dictionary)). **Enfadarse → ponerse bravo** is informal ([Yabla](https://espanol.yabla.com/lesson-The-meaning-of-bravo-in-Spanish-1273)). **Vararse** (*quedar varado*) is what a vehicle does when it breaks down, and the roadside fix is *desvararse*, labelled *Co.* (ICC; [es.wiktionary](https://es.wiktionary.org/wiki/varar)).

The ICC adds Colombian verbs a learner should recognize but that a rewriter should not insert. **Amañarse** (Co.) means to feel at home, where a Spaniard hears "to rig". *Cuadrar* (Am. inf.) is to set up a plan, and *trastearse* (Am.) is to move house. *Regar* and *botarse* mean to spill. *Molestar* (Co.) is an appliance acting up, *tirarse algo* (Co. inf.) is to wreck it, *voltear* (Co. pop.) is to hustle, and *cogerle la maña* is to get the hang of something. Slang verbs rest on learner sites and Caro y Cuervo citations. **Camellar** is "trabajar con esfuerzo" ([Radio Nacional](https://radionacional.co/cultura/historia-colombiana/los-colombianismos-una-riqueza-lexica-que-nos-une)), a word in which a Spaniard hears drug dealing ([Matador Network](https://matadornetwork.com/es/11-palabras-y-expresiones-colombianas-que-confundiran-un-espanol-si-o-si/)). *Rumbear*, *parchar* and *embarrarla* appear on several learner lists ([colombianspanish.co](https://colombianspanish.co/blog/colombian-spanish-learners-dictionary); [BaseLang](https://baselang.com/blog/vocabulary/colombian-idioms-and-slang)). So does *rajar (el examen)*, to fail an exam ([SpanishDict](https://www.spanishdict.com/translate/rajar)). *Emberriondarse*, to get angry, is mainly heard in Antioquia ([RAE DHLE](https://www.rae.es/dhle/emberriondar)). Spain's *pillar* ("to get it") is shared, since the ICC records "¿Sí pilla?" as *Am. inf.* Several often-cited pairs found no Colombian source and stay unverified: darse prisa → apurarse or afanarse, tardar → demorarse, volver → devolverse, ponerse de pie → pararse, tirar → botar (throw away), and suspender → perder la materia.

## Colombian slang must map register to register, from chévere to gonorrea

Core everyday slang is well attested and largely national. StoryLearning, Matador and Blu Radio agree on the meanings. **Chévere** and **bacano** mean cool, **parce / parcero** is a mate, **guayabo** a hangover and **plata** money. A **luca** is a thousand pesos, a **pola** a beer, **guaro** aguardiente and **tinto** black coffee. **¿Qué más?** and **¿quiubo?** mean "how's it going", and **de una** means "sure, right away". A **parche** is a group of friends or a plan, **vaina** is a thing or a hassle, a **sapo** is a snitch, **man / vieja** are guy and woman, and **paila** means tough luck ([StoryLearning](https://storylearning.com/learn/spanish/spanish-tips/colombian-slang-expressions); [Matador Network](https://matadornetwork.com/es/11-palabras-y-expresiones-colombianas-que-confundiran-un-espanol-si-o-si/); [Blu Radio](https://www.bluradio.com/nacion/las-10-expresiones-colombianas-que-no-se-encuentran-en-el-diccionario)).

The DA confirms these sayings as Colombian, sometimes shared with Venezuela, Ecuador or the Caribbean.

| Colombian | Meaning | Spain counterpart | DA marks |
|---|---|---|---|
| mamarle el gallo | tease someone; in Colombia also string someone along | tomar el pelo | Ho, Co, Ve ([DA mamar](https://www.asale.org/damer/mamar)) |
| estar en la olla | broke, or down | estar sin blanca | Co, RD ([DA olla](https://www.asale.org/damer/olla)) |
| me vale huevo | I don't care at all | me importa un pimiento | Co, Ec, **vulgar** ([DA valer](https://www.asale.org/damer/valer)) |
| echar los perros | flirt, court someone | tirar los tejos | shared with Mexico and others ([DA echar](https://www.asale.org/damer/echar)) |
| echar cepillo | flatter | hacer la pelota | Co ([DA echar](https://www.asale.org/damer/echar)) |
| lambón | suck-up; in Colombia also informer | pelota | ([DA lambón](https://www.asale.org/damer/lamb%C3%B3n)) |
| echar dedo | hitchhike | hacer autostop | Co ([DA echar](https://www.asale.org/damer/echar)) |
| pelado | kid, youngster; no "broke" sense for Colombia | chaval | ([DA pelado](https://www.asale.org/damer/pelado)) |

Some of the best-known sayings in Colombian media have no DA definition. Telemedellín calls **dar papaya**, with its proverb "a papaya puesta, papaya partida", a national commandment ([Telemedellín](https://telemedellin.tv/dichos-colombianos-mas-populares/)). **Hacer una vaca** is to pool money. It is safer than *hacer la vaca*, because in Peru "hacerse la vaca" means skipping class ([DA hacer](https://www.asale.org/damer/hacer)). **Poner los cachos** is Spain's *poner los cuernos*, and **qué oso / qué boleta** mean "how embarrassing" ([BaseLang](https://baselang.com/blog/vocabulary/colombian-idioms-and-slang)). Some phrases mean something else elsewhere in Latin America. Northern Colombian **hacer el dos** (do a favour) means defecating in several countries, Mexico included ([DA hacer](https://www.asale.org/damer/hacer)). Many items on "Colombian" dicho lists are general Spanish refranes, such as *hablar hasta por los codos*, *a otro perro con ese hueso*, *meter la pata* and *ponerse las pilas*, and they need no rewrite.

**The register ladder is the design constraint.** **Chimba** is Colombian colloquial for "an enjoyable or attractive thing" ([Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/chimba)), yet StoryLearning warns it can be considered vulgar. **Marica** works as "dude" among close friends but remains a homophobic slur outside that use ([Alturi](https://alturi.org/news_items/furia-marica-the-meaning-of-faggot-and-lgbt-rights-in-colombia)). **Gonorrea** is an extremely strong insult that came out of 1990s gang speech ([El Espectador](https://www.elespectador.com/opinion/del-papayo-la-gonorrea-columna-823284); [Worldcrunch](https://worldcrunch.com/in-the-news/tamales-to-gonorrhea-how-violence-shaped-colombian-spanish/amp/)). **Berraco** means brave, very capable or angry in Colombia, but a breeding boar in Spain ([UDEP](https://www.udep.edu.pe/castellanoactual/duda-resuelta-verraco-o-berraco/)). A rewrite should map Spain colloquial to Colombian colloquial (guay → chévere) and Spain crude to Colombian crude. It should never turn neutral text into slang or mild text into vulgar. That rules out "me importa un pimiento" → "me vale huevo" as a default, and *gilipollas* → *gonorrea* at any setting.

Regional tags can travel as notes. Chimba is strongest in the paisa region around Medellín ([Expat.com](https://www.expat.com/es/guia/america-del-sur/colombia/13941-modismos-en-colombia.html)). **Sumercé**, the respectful "you", survives in Boyacá and Cundinamarca, as in "qué pena con sumercé" ([Portafolio](https://www.portafolio.co/tendencias/sociales/que-pena-con-usted-la-expresion-en-colombia-que-confunde-a-los-extranjeros-601258)). *Yeyo* (feeling faint) comes from the Caribbean coast ([Blu Radio](https://www.bluradio.com/nacion/las-10-expresiones-colombianas-que-no-se-encuentran-en-el-diccionario)). Wiktionary gives *chino* = kid for Colombia without naming a city ([Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/chino)), and the Bogotá tag is unverified.

For "no way", the documented Colombian form is **ni de vainas**, a headword in the *Diccionario de colombianismos* ([Radio Nacional](https://radionacional.co/cultura/historia-colombiana/los-colombianismos-una-riqueza-lexica-que-nos-une)). **Ni de fundas** turned up in no Colombian or Mexican source, and Wiktionary has no entry for it ([Wiktionary 404](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/ni_de_fundas)). The researchers believe it is Mexican. Either way, it should not be presented as Colombian. For "right now", *ya mismo* rests only on blogs and Servientrega's "Ya Mismo" express brand, which treat it as "right away" ([filmadventure](https://filmadventure.blogspot.com/2011/03/word-for-now-in-colombia.html)). *Ahoritica* expresses true immediacy ([El Tiempo](https://www.eltiempo.com/cultura/gente/por-que-en-colombia-ahorita-no-significa-ahora-la-palabra-que-confunde-a-los-extranjeros-3524161)). *A la orden* is the courtesy of shops and service ([StoryLearning](https://storylearning.com/learn/spanish/spanish-tips/colombian-slang-expressions)). Several very common items found no source and stay unverified: *con gusto* (you're welcome), *listo*, *dale* and *hágale* (OK), *¡pilas!*, *cansón* and *gomelo* (Spain's *pijo*).

## Colombia is not Mexico with different slang

Colombia and Mexico share the pan-American replacements: carro, celular, papa, jugo, durazno, camarón, cobija, clóset, llanta, curita, aretes, brasier, tenis, control, audífonos, mesero, cuadra, direccional, marcador, manejar, prender, extrañar and echar los perros. On many everyday concepts, though, they choose different words, and a Mexico-mode rewrite inserts words that are wrong in Colombia. The sharpest case is **departamento**, Mexico's apartment, which in Colombia is one of the 32 administrative regions (ICC, *Co.*). Mexico's city bus, the **camión**, is only a truck in Colombia (ICC "camión de trasteos"). **Coger** is swapped in Mexico mode and must be kept in Colombia mode. Mexico's vulgar *mamar* (exaggerate) is not Colombian, while Colombian *mamar gallo*, once thought vulgar, is now ordinary ([El Tiempo](https://www.eltiempo.com/colombia/otras-ciudades/que-significa-la-expresion-mamar-gallo-y-de-donde-viene-508172); [DAMER mamar](https://www.asale.org/damer/mamar)). **Cruda**, Mexico's hangover, is not recorded for Colombia, which says *guayabo* ([DAMER cruda](https://www.asale.org/damer/cruda)). **Chido** is Mexico-only ([DAMER chido](https://www.asale.org/damer/chido)). The *¿me regala…?* request runs the other way: DAMER records it for central Colombia, not Mexico ([DAMER regalar](https://www.asale.org/damer/regalar)). DAMER also attests **chamba** in Colombia ([DAMER chamba](https://www.asale.org/damer/chamba)), but the characteristically Colombian word is *camello / camellar*, and the Colombia notes advise against producing *chamba*.

## The app's Colombia lists pick the right words but match them too loosely

The Colombia `Dialect` in `Flasher/Views/TranslateView.swift` (lines 266–291) is mostly well chosen, and most of its pairs match the research. Where each list runs determines how much a bad pair costs. The `spain` list runs through `localize()` on every English → Spanish translation (line 545) and on back-translations (line 682). The `plainer` list runs through `prepare()` on every Spanish → English request before it reaches the translator (line 855). The `phrases` and `reactions` lists run only inside `slangVersion()` (lines 411–417), which builds the separate local-wording card. So collisions in `spain` and `plainer` reach every user, and collisions in `phrases` and `reactions` only reach that card. All four lists share `replacing()` (lines 307–324). It matches each key case-insensitively wherever the key is bounded by non-letters, so a two-word key such as "de una" matches inside "de una vez". It applies one pair after another to the running text. It doesn't inflect, so a pair written as an infinitive or a singular misses every other form. Mexico mode covers every form through `MexicoWords`, while Colombia mode does so only for *echar de menos*. Finally, it doesn't fix articles, so "la resaca" becomes "la guayabo" in output, and on input "la plata" becomes "la dinero" and "el guayabo" becomes "el resaca".

The result is a set of collisions that produce broken Spanish.

| `piso → apartamento` (spain) | "vivo en el tercer piso" → "vivo en el tercer apartamento" | every translation |
| `pendientes → aretes` (spain) | "tareas pendientes" → "tareas aretes" | every translation |
| `conducir → manejar` (spain) | "conducir a una conclusión" → "manejar a una conclusión"; "conduzco" and "conduje" are never swapped | every translation |
| `de una → claro` (plainer) | "de una vez" → "claro vez"; "de una manera" → "claro manera" | every Spanish input |
| `pelado → muchacho` (plainer) | the participle in "he pelado las papas" | every Spanish input |
| `tinto → café` (plainer) | "vino tinto" → "vino café" | every Spanish input |
| `mono → rubio` (plainer) | monkey | every Spanish input |
| `chino → niño` (plainer) | "el idioma chino" | every Spanish input |
| `plata → dinero` (plainer) | silver: "un anillo de plata" | every Spanish input |
| `camello → trabajo` (plainer) | camel | every Spanish input |
| `pilas → cuidado` (plainer) | batteries: "necesito pilas" | every Spanish input |

| `ahora mismo → ya mismo` (phrases) | "ahora mismo estoy en casa" (currently), where *ya mismo* means "right away" | slang card |

The evidence supports a three-part fix. Move the courtesy words (*vale*, *de nada*, *de acuerdo*, *lo siento*, *de una*) onto the whole-sentence path that `swappingReactions()` already provides. Anchor sense-dependent nouns to context, as the sense-dependent table above does. Store an article with each noun pair whose gender changes.

The second set of problems is register and country. They sit on the slang card, which makes a slang target fair game, but not crude or foreign targets. **"qué increíble → qué chimba"** produces a word the app's own trap message (line 172) calls crude. **"no puede ser → no joda"** inserts *joda*, which the ICC marks vulgar, and *nojoda* is coastal (costeño), not national. **"de ninguna manera / ni hablar / ni de broma → ni de fundas"** uses a phrase with no Colombian source. In the Spain list, **"chaval → pelado"** matches Spain's colloquial register but carries the bald, peeled and participle readings, so neutral *muchacho* is the safer default. **"jersey → suéter"** has no Colombian source. The noun notes give *saco* from a travel guide, but *saco* collides with "sack" and "suit jacket", so the honest status is unverified. **"grifo → llave"** and **"cazadora → chaqueta"** are widely believed but unsourced in this research.

Outside the four lists, the **`cogerCO` trap** (lines 165–166) warns that *coger* "can be heard as sexual" in Colombia and recommends *tomar*. es.wiktionary, en.wiktionary and the ICC all directly contradict that warning. The **`conchaCO` trap** calls *concha* vulgar in Colombia, which the research could not support.

The largest omissions are the misreadings, none of which the lists cover: bocadillo, plátano, café solo → tinto, paro, canguro, Spain's "¡qué pena!" → *qué lástima*, "¿me pones…?" → "¿me regala…?", and a block on producing *bollo*. After those come the single-sense swaps in the noun section and the verbs *encender → prender* and *apetecer → provocar*.

## Conclusion

The research changes what the Colombia rewrite is for. The obvious design swaps Spain slang for Colombian slang. The evidence points instead to a misreading guard. A small set of Spain nouns and phrases (bocadillo, coche, plátano, tinto, paro, canguro, qué pena) silently change meaning in Colombia, and they deserve priority over any number of *chévere* swaps. The same evidence shows that Colombian vocabulary can't be derived from Mexico's. The two countries agree on a pan-American core but diverge on everyday objects, on *coger* and on courtesy phrases. Some of Mexico's targets, such as *departamento* and *camión*, are actively wrong in Colombia.

For the app, the bigger lesson is mechanical. The current word choices are mostly defensible, but a pair list matched by substring is unsafe for courtesy words and for any noun with a second meaning, because Spanish reuses short phrases (*vale*, *de nada*, *de acuerdo*, *de una*) inside ordinary sentences. Colombia mode will gain more from whole-reply matching, articles, verb forms and dropping three reactions than from new slang. The remaining uncertainty is register and frequency. Without the *Diccionario de colombianismos* or corpus data, the line between "should" and "optional" is a judgment from dictionary labels, and those two sources are what to check next.
