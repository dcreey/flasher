# Counterexamples to the "endpoint ahead → para, else por" rule

Rule under test: "Is there an endpoint ahead the event is headed for (destination, recipient, purpose, deadline, or a standard the thing is judged against)? Yes → para. No → por (cause, motive, route, means, exchange, duration, agent)." Listed exceptions: luchar/votar/preocuparse/esforzarse por, ir (a) por agua, estar por vs para, purpose + infinitive allowing both.

Grades: **F** = fetched page; **SN** = search snippet only; **L** = learner/forum site; **RK** = my own knowledge, unsourced. RK claims have no URL because none was found; they are judgements a native speaker or grammar would need to confirm.

Retrieval note: rae.es (DPD, NGLE online pages), core.ac.uk, uvadoc and munin.uit.no all blocked or failed (403, bot wall, DNS). So no NGLE or DPD text was fetched directly. NGLE and DPD claims below come from secondary pages.

## 1. Por with a clear endpoint ahead: closed list or productive?

### Takeaway
It is productive, not a closed list. One whole meaning class of *por* points at something wanted, sought, supported or competed for: the thing the action aims to get or help. It takes new nouns and verbs freely. History explains why: *por* was Spanish's purpose preposition until *para* (from *por + a*) took over purpose infinitives from the 18th century. So "por = no endpoint" is historically backwards for this layer. The rule can only absorb it after the fact by relabelling the goal a "motive", and that move can relabel any goal.

### Cited findings
- Diachrony: *para* arose from older *por* + *a* by chunking. From the 18th century *para* replaced *por* before infinitives and became the majority purpose marker. The authors present this as a gradual shift in variation with *por*, not a clean split (F, abstract page only; no numbers on the page) — [Torres Cacoullos & Bauman 2018, *Studies in Historical Ibero-Romance Morpho-Syntax*, Benjamins](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/allative-to-purposive-grammaticalisation-a-quantitative-story-of-/)
- *Ir por X* means 'en busca de' (to go and get) after verbs of motion. It is the preferred form in the educated standard (*Ve por ayuda* 'go get help'), and *a por* is also accepted, mainly in Spain. In the Americas *a por* sounds odd and plain *por* is used (*Voy por agua* 'I'm going for water') (F) — [Universidad de Piura, Castellano Actual, citing DPD 2005](https://www.udep.edu.pe/castellanoactual/duda-resuelta-voy-a-por/); same content in FundéuRAE wording (SN) — [alnavio.es summary](https://alnavio.es/ir-por-agua-o-ir-a-por-agua-la-rae-resuelve-la-duda)
- FundéuRAE says the pattern holds for motion verbs in general: *ir, venir, bajar…* + *por/a por* (SN), so it is not a single-verb exception — [alnavio.es](https://alnavio.es/ir-por-agua-o-ir-a-por-agua-la-rae-resuelve-la-duda)
- *Por* can carry a purpose reading, e.g. *Se casó por dinero* 'he married for money'. A UiT master's thesis (Bakken, 2014) has a section on the alternation between *por* and *para* (SN) — [munin.uit.no thesis](https://munin.uit.no/handle/10037/7616)
- A Spanish Ministry of Education teaching document lists *por* + infinitive as expressing purpose: *Viene al cine con nosotros por distraerse un poco* 'he's coming to the cinema with us to take his mind off things' (SN) — [redined.educacion.gob.es](https://redined.educacion.gob.es/xmlui/bitstream/handle/11162/72352/00820093000027.pdf?sequence=1)
- **The NGLE itself describes the governed-*por* verbs in endpoint terms.** It says *por* heads the governed complements of verbs denoting "tendencia o inclinación hacia algo" ('tendency or inclination towards something'), naming *votar, apostar, decantarse, decidirse, inclinarse, interceder, optar*. It contrasts *optar a* ('be eligible for / apply for') with *optar por* ('choose') (SN, NGLE text quoted in search snippet; rae.es fetch blocked) — [RAE, Gramática: complementos de régimen con para, por, sobre y tras](https://www.rae.es/gramática/sintaxis/complementos-de-régimen-con-las-preposiciones-para-por-sobre-y-tras). So the Academy's own wording puts an endpoint ("hacia algo") on these *por* verbs: a direct hit against the rule.
- DPD *apostar*: one sense is 'show confidence in, or opt for, someone or something' (*apostar por*) (SN) — [RAE DPD apostar](https://www.rae.es/dpd/apostar). FundéuRAE-style column lists *apoyar, optar, decidirse, confiar, abogar, favorecer, preferir, promover* as alternatives to overused *apostar por* (SN) — [El Caribe, Español urgente](https://www.elcaribe.com.do/opiniones/espanol-urgente/apostar-pero-tambien-apoyar-optar-decidirse-confiar-abogar/). *Abogamos por una vida más saludable* 'we advocate a healthier life' (SN) — [UCM Plataforma ELE](https://www.ucm.es/plataformaele/preposiciones)
- Bolinger wrote a whole article on the purpose overlap: "Purpose with por and para", *Modern Language Journal* 28(1), 1945, 15–22 (SN, citation only) — [cited in search result](https://linguisticsandlanguageteaching.blogspot.com/2011/02/clearing-up-native-english-speaking.html)

### Counterexample classes (RK unless marked)
Each item has an endpoint ahead (something to obtain, a beneficiary, a winner) yet takes *por*:

1. **Fetch or search (motion verb + por):** *ir/venir/salir/bajar/volver por pan* 'go/come/pop out/go down/come back for bread'; *mandar/enviar por el médico* 'send for the doctor'; *pasar por ti a las ocho* 'pick you up at eight'. The bread or the person is literally where the trip ends. The FundéuRAE source above confirms the class is open across motion verbs.
2. **Asking or looking for:** *preguntar por alguien* 'ask after/for someone'; *buscar por todas partes* is route, but *llamar por el camarero* ('call for the waiter', regional) is not.
3. **Support, advocacy, choice:** *votar por* (Latin America; Spain prefers *votar a*), *apostar por* 'bet on / back', *optar por* 'opt for', *decantarse por* 'lean towards', *inclinarse por* 'lean towards', *abogar por* 'advocate', *interceder por* 'intercede for', *velar por* 'look after', *hacer campaña por* 'campaign for'. The chosen option is the end the decision lands on.
4. **Fighting or competing for a prize:** *luchar/pelear/competir/pujar/disputarse por el título* 'fight/compete/bid/vie for the title'; *la carrera por la vacuna* 'the race for the vaccine'; *la batalla por Kiev* 'the battle for Kyiv'; *negociar por un aumento* 'negotiate for a raise' (some speakers use *para*). New nouns of contest take *por* freely, so this is productive.
5. **Desire nouns and adjectives:** *afán/ansia/deseo/sed/hambre/pasión/obsesión/interés/curiosidad por* + N or infinitive ('eagerness/craving/desire/thirst/hunger/passion/obsession/interest/curiosity for'); *ansioso/loco/impaciente por verte* 'eager/crazy/impatient to see you'. The desired thing is clearly ahead. The class is open: any new desire noun takes *por*.
6. **For the sake of (beneficiary):** *morir por la patria* 'die for one's country', *dar la vida por sus hijos* 'give one's life for one's children', *rezar por los enfermos* 'pray for the sick', *brindar por los novios* 'toast the bride and groom', *hacerlo por ti* 'do it for your sake'. The rule's recipient → *para* clause predicts *para*. Natives use *por*, and *lo hice para ti* means something else ('I made it for you', a gift).
7. **Exchange-as-goal:** *trabajar por dinero* 'work for money', *casarse por dinero* 'marry for money' (Bakken SN above). The money is what the action aims to get. The rule files it under exchange or motive, which works, but only after the fact.
8. **Por + infinitive as purpose,** especially after effort, avoidance or desire predicates: *esforzarse/afanarse/pugnar/morirse por aprobar* 'strive/struggle/be dying to pass'; *lo dije por no callar* 'I said it just to say something'; *por no molestar* 'so as not to bother anyone' (redined SN above).
9. **Estar por + infinitive = 'be inclined to', 'be tempted to':** *estoy por dejarlo todo* 'I'm half-minded to drop everything' — an intended action ahead. This sits beside *estar por* 'be pending' (*la casa está por limpiar* 'the house is yet to be cleaned') and, in the Americas, 'be about to' (*está por llover* 'it's about to rain'), which competes with Spain's *estar para*.

### Inferences
- The rule's motive slot absorbs every one of these only if "motive" includes "the wanted thing". But the wanted thing is the purpose. The rule then predicts nothing it couldn't also predict for *para*: *trabajo por mis hijos* and *trabajo para mis hijos* are both natural (see section 3). This is the after-the-fact problem in its clearest form.
- A better description has two productive *por* classes that the rule merges: (a) cause or motive behind the event, and (b) the thing sought, supported or competed for (the 'en busca de / en favor de' sense). (b) has an endpoint ahead by any ordinary reading. The current exceptions list (luchar, votar, preocuparse, esforzarse, ir por agua) covers only sample members of (b), not the class.
- Diachrony (Torres Cacoullos & Bauman) predicts exactly this: older purpose *por* survives where a desired or sought entity is named (a noun or a governed infinitive), while *para* won the free purpose clause. That is a principled residue, but it is a residue the endpoint test cannot predict.

### Gaps
- No corpus count found for how many verb and noun lemmas govern 'en busca de / en favor de' *por*. The NGLE governed-preposition section (rae.es page on *complementos de régimen con para, por, sobre y tras*) exists but returned 403.
- The full Bolinger 1945 and Bakken 2014 texts were not retrieved.

## 2. Para with no obvious endpoint: does "endpoint" stretch until it means nothing?

### Takeaway
*Para*'s non-endpoint uses are mostly a small set of fixed or semi-fixed frames. That supports "para is narrow". But several of them (opinion holder *para mí*, the comparison standard *para ser tan joven*, *para con*, *para siempre*, *para entonces*) fit the rule only because its endpoint was defined to include "recipient", "standard" and "deadline". With those included, "endpoint" means "any reference point". That would also cover *por* exchange (*gracias por*, *cambiar por*) and *por* rates (*por ciento*). *Para siempre* ('forever', a duration) directly contradicts "duration → por".

### Cited findings
- A cause/purpose overlap is noted in the literature: some *¿por qué?* 'why' questions can be answered with *para* phrases and vice versa. The source cites Pavón Lucero (2012) (SN) — [core.ac.uk PDF, study of Evita Perón's speech](https://core.ac.uk/download/233941398.pdf)
- A university Spanish manual summarises *para* as destination and purpose, "in order to" (SN) — [LibreTexts SPAN 301](https://human.libretexts.org/Workbench/SPAN_301%3A_Manual_de_gramatica_(Oechler)/01%3A_Descripcion/1.03%3A_Las_preposiciones_y_por_vs._para)
- There is a dedicated syntactic-semantic study of *para*, "Hacia una caracterización sintáctico-semántica de para" (*Anuario*, UVa, 2009). Its existence was seen in search; the text was not retrieved (SN) — [uvadoc](https://uvadoc.uva.es/bitstream/handle/10324/9492/ANUARIO-2009-25-HaciaUnaCaracterizacionSintacticoSemanticaDePara.pdf?sequence=1)

### Counterexample classes (RK)
| Example | Gloss | Endpoint? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Para mí, es un error.* / LatAm *Para mí que no viene.* | 'In my view it's a mistake' / 'I reckon he's not coming' | None in time or space; marks whose judgement counts | Fits only via "standard"; *para mí que* is an idiom |
| *Para ser tan joven, toca muy bien.* / *Para lo que cuesta, está bien.* | 'For someone so young, she plays very well' / 'For what it costs, it's fine' | A standard: the rule's own clause | Fits, but the "standard" clause was added to catch exactly this |
| *Es amable para con todos.* | 'He is kind towards everyone' | Target of an attitude | Borderline; *con* alone often suffices |
| *No estoy para bromas.* / *No es para tanto.* | 'I'm not in the mood for jokes' / 'It's not that big a deal' | Disposition, degree | Stretches; idioms |
| *Salió para no volver (jamás).* | 'He left, never to return' | An outcome seen in hindsight, not an aim | Mispredicts if endpoint means purpose; fits only if it means "what came after" |
| *para siempre* | 'forever' | Duration | **Contradicts** "duration → por" |
| *para entonces* / *para el lunes* | 'by then' / 'by Monday' | Deadline | Fits (deadline clause) |
| *para colmo* | 'to top it all' | None (discourse marker) | Fixed phrase |
| *para nada* | 'not at all' | None (negative intensifier) | Fixed phrase |
| *para que lo sepas* / *para variar* | 'just so you know' / 'for a change' (often ironic) | The purpose is the speech act, not the event | Fits only metacommunicatively |
| *¿Para qué?* vs *¿Por qué?* | 'what for?' vs 'why?' | Contrastive | Fits the rule well; the best case for it |
| *estar para* + infinitive (Spain) | 'be about to' | An impending event | Fits as an endpoint ahead |

### Inferences
- Para's leftovers (*para colmo, para nada, para siempre, no estar para, para mí que, no es para tanto*) look like a short, closed idiom list. On this point the "para is narrow" half of the claim is defensible.
- But the rule stays true only because "standard" and "recipient" were bundled into "endpoint". Once they are, the rule can't explain why *gracias por el regalo* ('thanks for the gift', the gift is the reference point) or *lo cambié por otro* ('I swapped it for another', the replacement is where the exchange lands) take *por*. The categories were drawn around the data, not predicted from the feature.
- *Para siempre* should be added to the exceptions list as a straight counterexample to "duration → por". (*Por siempre* exists but is literary or set-phrase, as in *por siempre jamás*.)

### Gaps
- No corpus figure found for the share of *para* tokens in fixed expressions. Candidate sources (CORPES XXI frequency of *para colmo, para nada, para siempre*) were not reachable.

## 3. Zone where both are natural with little or no meaning difference

### Takeaway
The overlap is real, recognised by grammarians, and centred on purpose and motive with human goals: por/para + infinitive after effort verbs, beneficiaries, and "for the good of". Authors who discuss it say the readings differ in concept (motive behind vs goal ahead) but are often interchangeable in practice. No corpus measure of the zone's size was found.

### Cited findings
- When the difference in value becomes almost imperceptible, *por* and *para* are interchangeable; another cited study holds that cause and purpose can co-occur in one preposition without making the two synonyms (SN) — [Nebrija, "El desafío preposicional en ELE"](https://revistas.nebrija.com/revista-linguistica/article/download/226/195/)
- Test pair *El niño se esfuerza por / para levantar la piedra* 'the child strains to lift the stone': the purpose reading survives with both. The author calls them interchangeable in language but not in concept (SN) — [Spanish-language study surfaced alongside Pavón Lucero citations](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/6137543.pdf) (attribution to this exact PDF is uncertain: the snippet was an aggregated search summary)
- Diachronically, purpose-infinitive *por* gave way to *para* gradually from the 18th century, with a long period of variation (F abstract) — [Torres Cacoullos & Bauman 2018](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/allative-to-purposive-grammaticalisation-a-quantitative-story-of-/)
- A Navarra (RILCE) article on purpose and prepositional infinitives says the purpose sense of *para* has weakened historically in some uses (SN) — [revistas.unav.edu RILCE](https://revistas.unav.edu/index.php/rilce/article/download/36799/34864/119930)

### Pairs natives accept both ways (RK)
- *Trabajar por / para el bien común* 'work for the common good'
- *Luchar por / para conseguir la igualdad* 'fight to achieve equality'. *Por* + noun is fixed; with an infinitive both occur.
- *Hacer lo posible por / para llegar a tiempo* 'do everything possible to arrive on time'
- *Rezar por / para que llueva* 'pray for rain'. With a noun, *por los enfermos* only; with a *que*-clause, both.
- *Lo hice por / para ayudarte* 'I did it to help you'. Felt difference: motive vs aim, often negligible.
- *Salí temprano por / para no encontrarme con él* 'I left early so as not to run into him'
- *Trabajo por / para mis hijos* 'I work for my children's sake' vs 'for my children as beneficiaries'
- *Votar por / para presidente* — different meanings: 'vote for (a candidate)' vs 'vote for president (the office)'. Not an overlap, but a trap the rule handles well.

### Inferences
- The overlap sits exactly where the rule's two halves meet (motive vs purpose). The rule says "both allowed" there, and that is honest. But the zone is a construction type (human goal + infinitive or *que*-clause after effort, desire or benefit predicates), not a list of a few verbs. It covers many verbs.

### Gaps
- No CREA/CORPES ratio for *por* + infinitive purpose vs *para* + infinitive in modern text was found. The Torres Cacoullos & Bauman chapter likely has historical ratios, but only its abstract was reachable.

## 4. Linguists arguing por/para can't be reduced to one feature

### Takeaway
The scholarly picture splits three ways. Reference grammars and teaching tradition list uses. Cognitive linguists argue for radial categories: related senses around a prototype, explicitly not a single feature. Diachronic work shows the split is a historical layering. No source found states the one-feature reduction as achievable. The one explicit critique found targets reducing *por* to [+cause].

### Cited findings
- Funes (2020, Chile), "Del espacio a la causa: el ítem polisémico de la preposición por": a prototype or radial-category account. It treats earlier reduction of *por* to a single value [+causa] as a problem, and says senses that diverge from the prototype remain dependent on it (SN; fetch returned an empty page) — [repositorioslatinoamericanos.uchile.cl](https://repositorioslatinoamericanos.uchile.cl/handle/2250/4395458)
- A classroom study taught intermediate learners *por/para* with a cognitive-linguistic description treating senses as related, against a group memorising a list of uses. The authors flagged confounds (SN) — [Informa / *Language Awareness* 10.1080/09658410802147345](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/09658410802147345)
- **Pinto & Rex 2006** — "The Acquisition of the Spanish Prepositions *Por* and *Para* in a Classroom Setting", *Hispania* 89(3): 611–622, DOI 10.2307/20063366. They ran a cross-sectional study of a corpus from four undergraduate levels of classroom-only learners. They argue that textbook explanations, especially those presenting the two prepositions **as opposites**, may not match learners' actual difficulties or the prepositions' functions in discourse (F, abstract page) — [University of St Thomas Research Online](https://researchonline.stthomas.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/The-Acquisition-of-the-Spanish-Prepositions/991015163652703691). This bears directly on a one-question binary rule, which is the most extreme "opposites" framing.
- Kissling, Tyler, Warren & Negrete (2018) say standard teaching presents a list of distinct meanings for each preposition in a one- or two-day unit. They propose a usage-based cognitive alternative (SN) — surfaced in the same search; no direct URL obtained.
- Counterpoint (L): a University of Kansas grammar page reports that framing *para* as prospective and *por* as retrospective "really clicks" with some learners. That framing is essentially the rule under test, and the page offers it as a teaching aid, not an analysis — [KU Acceso, por/para](https://acceso.ku.edu/gramatica/unidad5/por_para.shtml)
- Rubio (2015, U. Alabama, SECCLL) uses a cognitive framework to set out categories of meanings for each preposition, plus a corpus-based frequency count per category. Only the abstract is online and it gives no numbers (F) — [Georgia Southern Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.georgiasouthern.edu/seccll/2015/2015/29)
- Butt & Benjamin's *A New Reference Grammar of Modern Spanish* has a chapter "Prepositions para and por" (existence only; content not retrieved) (SN) — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/reference-grammar-of-spanish/prepositions-para-and-porlas-preposiciones-para-y-por/581B27DF4D0C6F03BB423EBE2F57F23B)
- Torres Cacoullos & Bauman: *para* = *por* + *a*, with gradual takeover of purpose. This implies the modern split is partly a historical accident of frequency, not one semantic feature (F abstract) — [pure.psu.edu](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/allative-to-purposive-grammaticalisation-a-quantitative-story-of-/)

### Inferences
- Even the unifying cognitive accounts use two separate radial networks, one per preposition, each with a prototype and extensions. None offers one binary feature that predicts the choice. The one-question rule goes beyond what any located scholarly source claims.
- The strongest argument against reduction is the overlap zone plus the 'en busca de / en favor de' *por* class: the same situation (a wanted goal ahead) is coded both ways, so no feature of the situation alone decides. The governing word and the history decide.

### Gaps
- Could not verify claims attributed to Lunn or Whitley (*Spanish/English Contrasts*). No Hispania article by Lunn on *por/para* surfaced. (Pinto & Rex was located and is cited above.) Treat any such attribution as unverified.
- RK, unverified: the structuralist tradition (e.g. Trujillo 1971, López 1970) did seek invariant meanings for each preposition. That is the opposite camp, and it should be checked before citing.
- NGLE §29.8 / DPD *por* and *para* entries: rae.es returned 403 for every fetch. Whether the NGLE frames *por/para* as lists or as unified values is unverified here.
- The Kissling, Tyler, Warren & Negrete (2018) usage-based *por/para* study was mentioned in one snippet but not located on a second search.

## 5. Frequency: fixed phrases and governed verbs vs free choice

### Takeaway
No usable figure was found. Rubio (2015) is the only located study that set out to count por/para by meaning category, and its numbers are not online. Learner-corpus work confirms *por* and *para* are the top error sources for learners, but gives no native-text breakdown.

### Cited findings
- Rubio 2015: corpus-based category frequencies promised; abstract only, no corpus named, no numbers (F) — [Georgia Southern](https://digitalcommons.georgiasouthern.edu/seccll/2015/2015/29)
- A UAB doctoral thesis on Chinese learners gives a frequency order de > a > en > con > por > para in its data, with *por* having the most errors and *para* the next most (SN) — [ddd.uab.cat thesis 2019](https://ddd.uab.cat/pub/tesis/2019/hdl_10803_670098/jigu1de1.pdf)
- A Nebrija interlanguage study has a table that seems to show about one-third *por* to two-thirds *para* in part of its data. The subset was unclear in the snippet, so treat this with caution (SN) — [revistas.nebrija.com](https://revistas.nebrija.com/revista-linguistica/article/download/1/3/4)
- Knoll (2018, Zagreb doctoral thesis) corpus-analyses a, de, en, por, para against Croatian. It may hold native frequency data (SN, existence only) — [core.ac.uk/works/45560348](https://core.ac.uk/works/45560348)

### Inferences (RK, hypotheses to test)
- *Por* token counts are likely inflated by very frequent fixed items: *por favor, por eso, por lo tanto, por ejemplo, por fin, por cierto, por lo menos, por supuesto, por si (acaso), por más que, por ciento*, and the passive agent (*escrito por*). If so, much of *por*'s breadth in tokens comes from fixed phrases a learner memorises anyway, and free *por*-vs-*para* choices are a minority of tokens. This is unverified and needs a CORPES n-gram check.
- *Para* + infinitive (purpose) is probably the single biggest *para* use. That would support "para is narrow and regular" in token terms.

- No CORPES XXI or CREA query results could be retrieved for the share of fixed, governed and free uses.

## Overall verdict (inference, combining the above)
- **Is the failure list closed?** Partly. On the *para* side, the leftovers are a short, mostly closed idiom list: *para colmo, para nada, para siempre, no estar para, para mí que, no es para tanto*. Of these, *para siempre* directly contradicts "duration → por". On the *por* side, the failures are **open-ended**. The 'in search of / in favour of / the prize competed for / the thing desired' class takes new members freely: motion verbs + *por*, contest nouns (*la carrera por…*), desire nouns (*afán por…*), choice verbs (*optar, apostar, decantarse por*). It is a productive sense, and history explains it (purpose *por* predates *para*). The rule copes only by calling the goal a "motive" after seeing the answer.
- **Is "para narrow and regular, por broad and mixed" right?** Mostly yes as a heuristic. *Para* has few senses and a small idiom tail. *Por* is broad, but part of that breadth is one coherent productive sense (the thing sought or supported) that the rule wrongly treats as exceptions. A fairer version: "*para* is narrow; *por* is broad, and one of its regular senses is 'for the sake of / in pursuit of'. Use *por* when the goal is a thing wanted or a cause backed rather than a place, deadline or recipient."
- **Suggested additions to the exceptions list:**
  - the motion-verb + *por* pattern (fetch), rather than just *ir por agua*;
  - choice and support verbs (*optar, apostar, abogar, velar, inclinarse, decantarse por*);
  - desire nouns and adjectives + *por* (*afán, ansia, interés, ansioso, loco por*);
  - contest nouns (*lucha, carrera, batalla por*);
  - 'for the sake of' *por* (*morir, rezar, brindar, hacerlo por alguien*);
  - *para siempre* (duration with *para*);
  - *para mí (que)* opinion;
  - the overlap zone as a construction (*por/para* + infinitive after effort, desire or benefit predicates), not a few verbs.
