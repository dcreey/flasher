# Scoring single rules for por vs para

Grades: **F** = fetched page or full text; **SN** = search snippet only; **L** = learner or forum site; **RK** = my own judgement, unsourced. Every scoring cell below is RK: I wrote the items and judged them, and no native speaker checked them. The catalogue IDs (P1–P14, R1–R26) are the numbering in `docs/research/reports/Por vs para rules challenge.md`.

## How do the candidate rules score on the full catalogue?

### Takeaway
None of the five candidates as stated gets more than about two-thirds of the 77 productive items right by its core criterion. A (endpoint) scores 66% and B (behind/ahead) 68%. C (given/new) scores 64%, E (why/what-for) 60% and D (inside/beyond) 58%. They fail in different ways. A is often **wrong**: it puts *para* on every goal that *por* goes after (15 wrong). B, C, D and E are mostly **undecidable**: 18 to 24 items where both clauses apply, or neither does, until you know the answer. **Of A–E as stated, B is the best:** 68% correct and only 3 wrong, but it pays for that with 20 undecidables. A reworded A, called **A′** here, scores 87% (91% on the plain productive items) with 6 wrong and 4 undecidable. A′ narrows *para* + noun to "delivered or measured" and keeps purpose for verb clauses only. A′ is in effect branch 1 of the challenge report's house rule (H), used as the only test with *por* as the default, which is why it ties with H. On 16 held-out items written before coding, A′ and H tie at 25% exceptions each and A has 50%. A′ is the best single rule found, but it is not better than H. It is a shorter way of stating H's *para* side.

### Cited findings
- Delbecque's account, as paraphrased by D'Antonio et al., gives *por* the view of the event "desde el punto de vista del trayecto o proceso, más que desde el destino final" (from the route or process rather than the final destination), covering space (*caminar por el parque*), cause (*lo hizo por amor*) and means (*por correo*). *Para* "introduce una meta o destinatario final" (introduces a goal or final recipient) (F) — [D'Antonio, Albiol-Martín & Priego-Casanova 2025, *Studi di Glottodidattica*](https://ojs.cimedoc.uniba.it/index.php/glottodidattica/article/download/2156/1943). The original is Delbecque (1996), "Towards a cognitive account of the use of the prepositions *por* and *para* in Spanish", in Dirven, Langacker & Taylor (eds.), *Cognitive Linguistics in the Redwoods*, pp. 249–318 (SN, citation only) — [Lam 2009 reference list](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.1080/09658410802147345). Candidate D's specific wording ("in contact with / traversed by" vs "projected beyond") appears in no fetched source. I reconstructed it (RK).
- Meta-operational account (Adamczewski; Matte Bon 2016). *Para* is "Fase I" because it "propone un dato nuevo" (proposes a new datum). *Por* is "Fase II" because it "no introduce ningún dato nuevo, es preexistente", "por eso, se afirma que por expresa la causa, pues esta siempre precede al efecto" (that is why *por* is said to express cause, since a cause always precedes its effect) (F) — [D'Antonio et al. 2025](https://ojs.cimedoc.uniba.it/index.php/glottodidattica/article/download/2156/1943).
- The same paper analyses *Trabajo para mi jefe* as "dato nuevo" yet calls the boss "el motor de la acción" (the driving force of the action), which is the role its own account gives *por*. The source itself shows candidate C fitting an item only after the fact (F) — [D'Antonio et al. 2025](https://ojs.cimedoc.uniba.it/index.php/glottodidattica/article/download/2156/1943).
- In purpose contexts, *para* is "la forma neutra" and *por* "nunca presenta el fin de forma contundente" (never presents the aim forcefully). The paper gives *Pasa por la ciudad por/para no pasar por la Vía de Cintura* and *me he quedado en la oficina por terminar mi trabajo* as items where both prepositions work (F) — [D'Antonio et al. 2025](https://ojs.cimedoc.uniba.it/index.php/glottodidattica/article/download/2156/1943).
- Patin (2016) reports that "la mayoría de los lingüistas es unánime" (most linguists agree) that purpose is less fixed in *por* than in *para*. Its pairs swap "prácticamente sin cambio de sentido" (with practically no change of meaning): *Trabajo por/para ganar el pan* (Morera Pérez 1988), *Me voy por/para no verla* (CLAVE). It also has the noun pair *Cerrado por descanso del personal* vs *Cerramos para descanso de los clientes* (De Bruyne 1999) (F) — [Patin 2016, marcoELE 22](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/921/92153510015/92153510015.pdf).
- Regimes such as *interesarse por*, and chunks such as *por favor*, *gracias por*, *por cierto*, are described as lexicalised, belonging to the norm rather than the system, forming "listas no finitas" (open-ended lists) for which "difícilmente podemos ofrecer a los estudiantes reglas" (we can hardly give students rules) (F) — [Patin 2016](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/921/92153510015/92153510015.pdf).

### Inferences

**Method (RK).** I wrote 93 sentences: 56 plain productive (P), 13 governed but semantically motivated (G), 8 overlap-zone items where both forms are acceptable (O), and 16 lexical or fixed items (X). Every catalogue ID from P1–P14 and R1–R26 appears at least once. For each row I first described X neutrally (column 6) and then derived each rule's output from that description. Codes:
- **Y**: the rule's core criterion gives the attested form.
- **L**: correct only because the rule's parenthetical list names the use. This counts as an exception for teaching.
- **N**: the core gives the wrong form.
- **U**: undecidable. Both clauses apply, or neither does, or I could fit it only by knowing the answer.

Each half of a minimal pair is its own row. In overlap rows, any acceptable output scores Y, and the notes say whether the rule predicts the alternation at all. Row 60 (*para/por tres semanas*) and the overlap pairs not sourced from Patin are my judgements (RK).

**Candidates as scored (core criterion only):**
- **A, endpoint.** *Para* if the event is headed for an endpoint ahead; otherwise *por*. *Por* is the default.
- **A′, delivered or measured (my rewording).** *Para* if X is where the action is **delivered** (destination, receiver or user, deadline, intended use) or **measured** (yardstick, viewpoint). With a verb (infinitive or *que*-clause), *para* also marks the result the action is **meant** to bring about. Otherwise *por*. The change from A: a wanted noun (bread, independence, a candidate, money) is not an endpoint unless it is a place, receiver, deadline, use or yardstick.
- **B, behind vs ahead.** *Por* = what exists before or during the action and pushes, carries or is exchanged in it. *Para* = what the action produces, reaches or serves. Both are positive values, so there is no default.
- **C, given vs new (Matte Bon).** *Por* = a datum already there; *para* = a datum projected. The scoring found two readings: the **referent** is already given, or the **relation** already exists. A cell is Y or N only when both readings agree, and U when they split.
- **D, inside vs beyond (Delbecque, reconstructed).** *Por* = landmark the process is in contact with or passes through, including cause as the source of the path. *Para* = landmark projected beyond the process.
- **E, why vs what for.** Applied through English questions to avoid circularity. *Por* if X answers "why / because of what / through what"; *para* if it answers "to what end / to whom (as receiver) / by when / to where".
- **H, the challenge report's house rule (baseline).** Lexicon first. Then *para* = delivered or measured; *por* = gone after (fetched, fought for, chosen, for whose sake); otherwise *por* = driven or carried (cause, route, means, exchange, rate, span, agent).

**What the tallies say (RK).**
- **The overlap rows cannot tell the candidates apart.** Every rule outputs an acceptable form. Only B, C and E also predict that *por* is possible there, because they have two construals. A, A′ and D predict only *para*. That is a point for B, C and E on explanation, but not on accuracy.
- **A fails by being wrong, the others by being empty.** A's 15 wrong answers are almost all the pursuit class: *voy por pan*, *luchar por*, *la carrera por*, *votar/optar por*, *loco por verte*, *morir/rezar/brindar por*, the exchange items (*por el libro*, *pesos por dólares*) and *se casó por dinero*. B rarely gives a wrong answer (3), but it is undecidable on 20 items. "What the action reaches" and "the desired thing that pushes it" are both in its own statement, so on every pursuit item it gives both answers. B moves A's errors into undecidables rather than fixing them.
- **C's two readings disagree on a quarter of the items.** On the referent reading, C handles choice from a closed set well (*optar por* among given options, *morir por la patria*). On the same reading it gets the recipient (*para usted*), anaphoric *para entonces* and *para eso* wrong. On the relation reading it is roughly B. It gets the yardstick and viewpoint uses (*alto para su edad*, *para mí*) wrong on both readings, because the age and the speaker are already there.
- **D is strongest on space and time and weakest everywhere else.** It is perfect on route, area, span and deadline, which is where it came from. It has no answer for exchange, rate, agent, function, yardstick or beneficiary (24 undecidable).
- **E is circular in Spanish and leaky in English.** English "for" covers *pagar por* and *votar por*, so "to what end / for whom" pulls toward *para* on exchange and pursuit items (10 wrong). Time questions ("when?", "how long?") are in neither list.
- **A′ removes A's pursuit errors with one move.** With a noun, *para* needs X to be a place, receiver, deadline, use or yardstick, and anything merely wanted takes the *por* default. Of A's 15 errors, that move turns 9 into hits and 2 into undecidables (*votar por*, *loco por verte*). It leaves 4 wrong: the three for-whose-sake rows (*morir/rezar/brindar por*) and *luchar por que*. In the for-whose-sake rows the beneficiary counts as receiving the effect, the same reading that makes *malo para la salud* correct. Its remaining failures are listed in the next section.
- **H, the house rule, has no wrong answers but 11 list-rescued items** (for whose sake, exchange, rate, dropping in, taken as) and one undecidable (*estudia para médico*: delivered or gone after?). H scores well partly because its branch 3 is a catalogue. Counting list rescues as exceptions, A′ (13% exceptions) and H (16%) are about level, and A′ is the shorter statement.
- **Test-set bias.** The set over-samples hard cases (the pursuit class has 13 rows), so these percentages understate every rule's coverage of running text. That coverage is unmeasured, since no corpus count by use exists in the notes.

## Which candidate is best, and what is its exception list?

### Takeaway
**Best rule, in one sentence: use *para* when X is where the action is delivered (destination, receiver or user, deadline, intended use) or measured (yardstick, point of view), or, before a verb, the result the action is meant to bring about; use *por* everywhere else.** Its exceptions are **partly open**. Three classes take new members freely: *por* for the person or cause for whose sake something is done, desire predicates + infinitive, and *para* + goal-state noun. The rest are closed lists of constructions and fixed phrases, all set out below. So it has the subjunctive-style shape (a default *por*, a marked *para* with a test, and a list) only in part, because a trigger list is closed and this residue is not. A′ is H's branch 1 used on its own. It tied H on a held-out check.

### Cited findings
- The *por* regimes that A′ leaves undecidable are the ones the NGLE groups as verbs of "tendencia o inclinación hacia algo" (tendency or inclination towards something): *votar, apostar, decantarse, decidirse, inclinarse, interceder, optar* (SN, via the challenge pass) — [RAE, Gramática, complementos de régimen](https://www.rae.es/gramática/sintaxis/complementos-de-régimen-con-las-preposiciones-para-por-sobre-y-tras).
- Purpose *por* before infinitives is the older layer. *Para* replaced it from the 18th century onwards (F, abstract) — [Torres Cacoullos & Bauman 2018](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/allative-to-purposive-grammaticalisation-a-quantitative-story-of-/). This is why A′ makes *para* the default for verb-purpose and accepts *por* there.
- English-speaking learners produce *para* in 64–68% of *por/para* tokens, and 83% at intermediate level in Lafford & Ryan, as re-tabulated by [Jiménez 2017](https://revistas.nebrija.com/revista-linguistica/article/download/1/3/4). A rule that makes *para* the marked form that must be justified targets that error. This was already argued in the first report.

### Inferences
**Exception list for A′ (RK).** In brackets: the row numbers it failed on in the table.
1. **OPEN. *Por* + person or cause for whose sake** [33, 34, 35, 36; held-out h01, h15]: *morir por la patria*, *rezar/interceder por los enfermos*, *brindar por los novios*, *sacrificarse por sus hijos*, *hacerlo por ti*. A′ is plainly wrong here. A beneficiary counts as receiving the effect, the same reading that makes *malo para la salud* correct, so A′ predicts *para*. Contrast: *para* + person = they receive the thing or are affected by it (*es para ti*, *malo para la salud*). The repair is required, not optional. Ask a second question: "do they get it, or is it done for their sake?"
2. **Semi-open. Choice verbs** [29]: *votar por* (Latin America; Spain *votar a*). *Optar, apostar, decantarse por* already pass under A′ (row 30), since an option is not a receiver.
3. **OPEN. Desire predicates + infinitive** [32; h03]: *loco/ansioso/impaciente por* + infinitive, *morirse por* + infinitive.
4. **Closed (tied to the *por* regimes). *Por que* / *porque* + subjunctive after a *por* regime** [90]: *luchar por que haya paz*.
5. **Closed. *Pasar por*** "drop in" [63].
6. **OPEN. *Para* + goal-state noun** [75, 77; h04]: *estudiar para médico*, *para descanso de los clientes*. A′ predicts *por* here because the noun is not a place, receiver, deadline or use. This is the mirror image of *luchar por la independencia*, and no single feature separates the two.
7. **Closed. Constructions** (lexical, listed by any rule): *estar por* + infinitive (Americas "about to"; general "pending"), *quedar por* + infinitive, *para siempre*, *salir para no volver*, *para con*, *no estar para*, *para colmo*, *para mí que*.
8. **Closed. Fixed *por* phrases**, harmless because *por* is the default: *por favor, por fin, por si acaso, por más que, hablar por hablar, por mí, dos por tres, por ciento*.

**Why A′ and not B or C (RK).** A′ still keeps one asymmetry: *para* has a positive test and *por* is the rest. Every *por* use the catalogue lists separately (rate, multiplication, *tener por*, fixed phrases, exchange) therefore comes free, and these are many of *por*'s high-frequency tokens. B and C give *por* a positive value, so each of those uses needs to be justified on its own, and they come out undecidable. A′'s cost is that it is disjunctive. "Delivered or measured, or the meant result of a verb" is really three clauses and a five-item *para* list (place, receiver, deadline, use, yardstick), not one feature.

## Is there published scoring or native-speaker judgement data?

### Takeaway
I found no published attempt to score candidate single rules against the uses of *por/para*, and no native-speaker forced-choice or acceptability study of the overlap zone. Two kinds of data are all there is: learner studies and teaching-method comparisons. One heritage-learner study shows that heritage speakers choose correctly without stated rules.

### Cited findings
- Heritage vs non-heritage learners (*Entrehojas* 3(1)): cloze and grammaticality-judgement tasks plus explanations of choices. Heritage learners showed automaticity and procedural knowledge, while non-heritage learners relied on explicit rules (SN; the article page redirected and the mirror returned 403) — [Entrehojas, "Heritage Language Learners and Automaticity: The Use of 'por' and 'para'"](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.5206/entrehojas.v3i1.6135).
- D'Antonio et al. say L2 learners "(y en ocasiones el nativo promedio también)" (and sometimes the average native speaker too) "a menudo no perciben con precisión qué valor tienen" (often do not perceive precisely what value the prepositions have) in cause/purpose contexts. This is an assertion, not data (F) — [D'Antonio et al. 2025](https://ojs.cimedoc.uniba.it/index.php/glottodidattica/article/download/2156/1943).
- Patin's error corpus comes from translation exercises by French-L1 university students. It concludes that the binary system "resulta inoperante" (stops working) when students need nuance, from B2 to C2 (F) — [Patin 2016](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/921/92153510015/92153510015.pdf).
- Rubio (2015) set out categories of meaning with a corpus frequency count per category, but only the abstract is online and it gives no numbers (F, abstract; from the challenge notes) — [Georgia Southern Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.georgiasouthern.edu/seccll/2015/2015/29).
- Lam (2009): a cognitive description vs a list. The cognitive groups confused the two prepositions less in free writing, with confounds the author names (SN) — [Lam 2009, *Language Awareness*](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/09658410802147345). Kissling et al. (2018) proposed two polysemy networks, each with a spatial core (SN) — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/232791566.pdf); the fetch returned 403.
- My web searches for native-speaker *por/para* elicitation or forced-choice studies returned only unrelated methodology papers and Portuguese learner guides (L). No relevant source was found.

### Inferences
- Without native judgement data, every "both acceptable" claim and every U/N call in the table is a single annotator's judgement (RK). Three cheap checks would test the scores: give native speakers the 93 items as a forced choice with a "both" option, have two annotators code the items blind, and score the candidates on new held-out items.

### Gaps
- Delbecque 1996, Matte Bon 2016, Bolinger 1945 and the Entrehojas article were not read in full.
- No data on how often natives accept both forms in the overlap zone.

## Is a single high-level rule defensible for teaching?

### Takeaway
**Only partly, even in the subjunctive's sense.** A′ has the shape of the assertion test: one test for the marked form (*para*), a default (*por*), and a list. On the 77 productive items it gets 87% by its core alone. But three of its exception classes are open (for whose sake, desire + infinitive, *para* + goal-state noun), and the first needs a second question rather than a list. So the honest teaching object is two questions plus a closed list, not one rule. That object is the challenge report's house rule with its branches reordered. **No, if "single rule" means one feature that predicts the choice.** Every one-feature candidate (B, C, D, E) is undecidable on a quarter or more of the items, and A's single feature is wrong on a fifth. Even A′ is a disjunction of clauses rather than one feature.

### Cited findings
- The meta-operational authors themselves conclude for eclecticism, saying that beginners may do better with simple rules or lists (F, as summarised in the challenge report) — [D'Antonio et al. 2025](https://ojs.cimedoc.uniba.it/index.php/glottodidattica/article/download/2156/1943).
- Patin calls the binary opposition "necesaria para asentar las bases del aprendizaje inicial" (necessary to lay the groundwork for initial learning) but inoperative at higher levels. Quoting Lenarduzzi, it says the teacher's job is to take the drama out of the contrast (F) — [Patin 2016](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/921/92153510015/92153510015.pdf).
- Textbook explanations that present the two prepositions as opposites may not match learners' difficulties (F, abstract) — [Pinto & Rex 2006, *Hispania*](https://researchonline.stthomas.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/The-Acquisition-of-the-Spanish-Prepositions/991015163652703691).

### Inferences
- **How the analogy holds and where it breaks (RK).**
  - **What matches.** The assertion test picks out the marked mood with one test, leaves the subjunctive as the default, and adds a trigger list. A′ does the same with *para* as the marked form and *por* as the default. Its lexical residue, groups 7 and 8 above, is closed in the same way trigger lists are.
  - **What breaks.**
    - *Para*'s test is a five-way disjunction, not one feature.
    - One exception is productive and semantic, not lexical: *por* for the person or cause for whose sake something is done. It needs its own question.
    - Purpose with an infinitive is a real zone of free choice. The subjunctive has nothing like it, apart from a few verbs where both moods are possible.
- **Recommended teaching object (RK).**
  - **Lead with A′ as the rule:** "Is X where it is delivered, by when, what it is for, or what it is measured against? Then *para*. Otherwise *por*."
  - **Add one sub-question for people:** "Do they get it (*para*), or is it done for their sake (*por*)?"
  - **Teach "por = going after" as the main family *por* covers,** not as a list of exceptions: fetch, fight, choose, desire.
  - **Keep a construction list:** *estar/quedar por*, *para siempre*, *para con*, *no estar para*, *para colmo*.
  - **Treat purpose + infinitive separately:** *para* is the default and *por* is never marked wrong.
  - **Compared with H:** this is H. A′'s test is H's branch 1, the sake sub-question is part of H's branch 2, and "*por* = going after" is the rest of branch 2. The result is a presentation order: lead with the *para* test, and make *por* the default with one named family. It is not a new rule. Exceptions: 13% (A′) vs 16% (H) on the main set, and 25% each on the held-out set.
- **Confidence (RK).** Moderate on the ranking of A, B, C, D and E, because their failure patterns are structural and do not depend on borderline calls. Low on the exact percentages: there is one annotator, the item set is designed rather than sampled, and A′ was fitted after the fact.

### Gaps
- No experiment compares a single-rule presentation with networks or lists on production tasks. The Kissling et al. follow-up found an advantage for networks on multiple-choice tests but not on fill-in-the-blank (SN, from the challenge report) — [Benjamins, pl.22005.kis](https://benjamins.com/catalog/pl.22005.kis).
